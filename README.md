# Why Are We Here? Reading Code in the Age of AI Writing
## Introduction
In 2026, I can type a sentence into a text box and an AI will produce working code in seconds. As a student who majored in Network and New Media during my undergraduate studies, I once thought programming was just a shallow, mandatory course—something to survive, not to master. But as I look at my career path in internet operations and my internship experience in gaming companies, I realize the question is no longer "can a machine write this?" but "how do I know if what the machine wrote is actually any good?"
## The Gap Between Writing and Reading
This course's question resonates deeply with me: if a machine writes the code, someone still has to know whether it is any good. That someone is me. This is what Wagner calls the shift from "code writers to code curators" [1]. To be that someone, I do not need to be a software engineer, but I must be able to *read* the machine's logic. I need to spot when a generated script has a hidden bias, when it contains a logical loophole, or when it simply doesn't do what I asked. The shift is profound: the value of programming is moving from "writing syntax" to "editing and directing intent."
## My Own Practice:Specificity from Experience
During my internship at a gaming company, I wanted to create a script that could scrape trending keywords from the internet, or automatically sort videos related to a specific topic by their play count. I quickly realized that if I only relied on AI to generate the code, I could never fully trust it. If the sorting algorithm was slightly wrong, the data would be useless for my operational decisions. I had to understand the basic logic of loops, conditionals, and data structures myself to check the AI's output and tweak it until it was precise. As Mirolo and Izu found, even novices who recognise a quality issue may still misjudge it—judgment requires training [2]. That experience taught me that in the age of AI, a designer or operator's ability to *specify* and *verify* is more valuable than ever.
## What It Takes to Be That Someone
To be the person who can judge the machine's code, I believe we need several key qualities. First, **judgment**—the ability to tell right from wrong in the output. Second, **creativity**—knowing what problem is worth solving in the first place. Third, **needs awareness**—understanding what the user or business truly requires. Fourth, **knowledge reserves**—the foundational logic that allows us to spot errors. Beyond these, I would add **logical decomposition**—breaking a complex goal into executable steps—and **critical thinking**—questioning the AI's assumptions and its hidden biases. Chuan et al. show that integrating AI literacy into programming education significantly enhances students' ability to critically evaluate solutions [3]. Without these, we are just passengers in our own creative process, unable to tell if the destination is where we wanted to go.
## The Historical Rhyme
In the 1960s, Frieder Nake and Georg Nees created computer-generated art and were accused of replacing artists. Photography did not end painting; synthesisers did not end musicianship. The tools changed, but the human intention behind them remained essential. This time feels different in scale, but the pattern is familiar. Yet reports suggest AI-generated code contains roughly 1.7 times as many issues as human-written code, with logic errors and security flaws being particularly prominent [4]. AI will not replace designers who understand their medium. It will only replace those who do not.
## Conclusion
I am here because I want to be the author, not just the operator. I am here because I want to know why the machine wrote that line, and whether it serves my purpose. Programming is not about typing faster than a machine; as Beattie argues, code is an expressive medium, not just a technical chore [5]. It is about thinking clearly enough to command it—and caring enough to verify it. That is what survives.I am still learning what that looks like in practice, but this course is where I begin.
